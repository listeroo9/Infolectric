Electrical Fundamentals

Wire Sizing

Wire sizing is the process of choosing a conductor cross-sectional area (AWG or mm²) so that the wire can safely carry the expected current (ampacity) without excessive heating. Proper sizing accounts for the expected continuous current, the wire material (copper or aluminum), insulation type, ambient temperature, and installation conditions.

It matters because undersized wires increase resistance, produce voltage drop over distance, reduce efficiency, and can overheat insulation leading to failure or fire. Designers use ampacity tables and account for run length and voltage drop to select the minimum safe wire size for a circuit.

Current

Current is the flow of electric charge measured in amperes (A); it represents how much load is being drawn through a conductor at any moment. In practical systems, current determines how large conductors and protective devices must be because the conductor must carry the expected load without exceeding its ampacity.

Excess current produces heat and can damage wiring and equipment, which is why circuits include fuses or circuit breakers sized to interrupt fault currents. Understanding both steady-state and fault currents helps select protective devices and ensure safe operation.

Voltage

Voltage is the electrical potential difference measured in volts (V) that drives current through a circuit; think of it as the pressure that moves charge. Different systems (USB, automotive, household mains) use different nominal voltages, and devices are rated to operate at specific voltages.

Voltage affects insulation requirements, clearances, and transformer or converter selection; it also interacts with current to determine power (P = V × I). Maintaining correct voltage levels and limiting voltage drop along conductors is essential for reliable device operation and safety.

Power & Safety

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer measured in watts (W) and is related to voltage and current by P = V × I. Calculating power lets you size supply circuits and estimate energy use — for example, converting appliance wattage into the current a circuit must provide.

Safety combines correct power handling with protective measures: proper grounding, correct wire sizing, overcurrent protection, and adherence to codes. Managing short-circuit currents, ensuring adequate insulation and ventilation, and using certified protective devices reduce the risk of overheating, fire, and electric shock.
